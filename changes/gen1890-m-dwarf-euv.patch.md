**Headline:** The extreme ultraviolet of a star is now the measured share of its X-rays (6.6 times for the Sun, about 0.9 for Proxima Centauri), so the small stars no longer strip their planets' air with an EUV 30 times the measured one.

### Changed
- **The EUV to X-ray ratio follows the X-ray flux at the star's surface (GEN.1890).** The star's EUV was `log L_EUV = 4.80 + 0.860 log L_X`, a function of luminosity alone, which gave every star about 9 to 10 times its X-rays in the EUV: 10 for the Sun today (measured 6.6, Ribas et al. 2016) and 8.9 for Proxima Centauri (measured 0.9, and about 1.1 in Ribas et al. 2017), so Proxima b was lit by 4,797 erg/s/cm2 of XUV in the generator against the measured 307 (GEN.1889). The EUV is now `F_EUV / F_X = 425 F_X^-0.42` with the fluxes at the star's surface (Chadney et al. 2015, Eq. 2a), which gives the Sun 6.5, Proxima 0.88 and a saturated Sun 0.26: a more active star, or a cool one with a small surface, makes less EUV for each X-ray. Stored star XUV (`l_xuv_w`, `xuv_fluence_j`), every body's XUV exposure index and what rests on it change; the XUV of a saturated G star falls from 3.3e-3 to 9.3e-4 of its luminosity, of a late M dwarf from 7.3e-3 to 1.4e-3. Registry row AB2, references R5378 and R5379 (Ribas et al.).
