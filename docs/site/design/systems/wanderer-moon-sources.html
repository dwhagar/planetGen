GEN.560 (moon parts): ejection survival of moons and circumplanetary disks of isolated planetary-mass objects

docs/design/systems/wanderer-moon-sources.md

Status: research, no behaviour change. It closes GEN.560 with the black hole part (black-hole-formation-sources.md) and the interstellar size law (small-body-size-laws.md section 1) already written. It feeds GEN.558 (moons of wanderers: ejection survivors and isolated-disk moons as table rows) and the moon rule of unified-star-like-objects.md section 3.6.

Method: each paper was downloaded as the arXiv PDF and read in the text. "Quoted" is copied from the paper; "own reading" is a plain restatement. Source states as in the small-body note: READ is a number found in the paper this session; 2ND-HAND is a number a read paper quotes from another; GAP is a number no paper read here gives. Raw papers stay out of the repository. The plan's two recalled sources ([R1032], "[R1998]") are checked in section 3: the first exists as recalled, the second is not the paper that has the numbers.

1. Moons that survive the ejection of their planet

Case Number Source State
Earth-mass planet with a lunar-mass moon at 30 Earth radii, scattered by a Jovian planet; 2700 runs of 10^6 yr 123 runs (4.6%) end with the Earth-Moon pair ejected still bound; 90 (3.3%) eject the Earth alone [R1032], ApJ [R1032], section 3 READ
Same median eccentricity 0.13 and semi-major axis 30 Earth radii of the ejected pairs; tidal heat up to 4.2e22 erg/s (100 times radiogenic) for a rocky planet same READ
Same free-floating binary planets: 7e8 in the Galaxy for 1e10 stars, a space density of 9e-4 per pc^3 (their assumptions) same READ
Three Jupiter-mass planets, innermost at 3 AU, 100 test-particle moons per ejected planet about 85% of moons at the Galilean distances survive; moons survive out to beyond 200 planetary radii (0.1 AU in that setup); just over 25% of runs keep all or most of their moons [R3640], MNRAS [R3640], section 3 READ
Same survival fraction against the moon's initial distance: constant at small distances, then a power law; the least-squares index is -0.607 +- 0.017 (an index of -1/2 fixed gives a worse fit) same READ
Planet-planet scattering of two or three planets (circular restricted three-body plus N-body) the probability that the minimum planet-planet distance exceeds 0.1 R_Hill is 48% and exceeds 0.01 R_Hill is 82% (fiducial runs, m1/M about 1e-2, m1/m2 = 10); regular Solar System moons orbit at about 1e-2 R_Hill, so a significant fraction survive; a moon inside 1e-2 R_Hill survives with at least 70% for any initial separation [R2031], ApJ [R2031], section 4 and conclusions READ
Same survival decreases somewhat as the planet mass and the mass ratio m1/m2 fall; the finite size of the planets does not change the statistic while the planet radius is far below the minimum distance same READ

Own reading for the draw: the survival of a moon is a function of its distance to its planet in Hill radii at ejection, flat at about 0.7 to 0.85 inside 1e-2 R_Hill and falling as a power law of index about -0.6 outside it (Rabago and Steffen), reaching about 0.48 at 0.1 R_Hill (Huang and Lai). The two papers agree inside 1e-2 R_Hill (0.82 and 0.85). They differ in scale on the tail because one fixes the planet's distance from its star and the other the planet separation in Hill units; the wider range wins (the standing rule for two sources in question). Debes and Sigurdsson's 4.6% is a different quantity: the share of scatterings of an Earth-mass pair that eject the pair intact, for one set of start distances, not a survival fraction of moons that start bound.

2. Circumplanetary disks of isolated planetary-mass objects

Case Number Source State
Disk fraction of free-floating planetary-mass objects (M9 or later) in IC 348, Spitzer 3.6 and 4.5 um stacked six with disks plus three uncertain of the sample: 46 +- 13/12 percent; "comparable to more massive brown dwarfs"; the objects keep disks for several million years [R4016], MNRAS [R4016], abstract READ
Same in NGC 1333 5 of 12 planetary-mass objects show excess: 42%, large uncertainty; the disk fraction does not decline substantially with falling mass in the substellar range [R3969], abstract READ
OTS 44, about 12 Jupiter masses, ALMA band 6 unresolved detection; dust mass 0.07 to 0.63 Earth masses by the empirical correlations; one scaling of dust mass with central mass holds from brown dwarfs to planetary masses [R0293], ApJ Letters [R0293], abstract READ
2MASS J04414489+2301513 (about 0.05 Msun) disk radius 15 to 30 AU, first resolved substellar disk [R3756], quoted by [R0293] 2ND-HAND
Number of free-floating planets known with a disk (to 2017) four, listed with their masses as Cha 1109-7734 (8 Mjup), OTS 44 (12), J02265658-5327032 (13) and Proplyd 133-353 (at most 13, [R1320]) [R0293], introduction READ

Own reading for the draw: a planetary-mass object formed on its own keeps a disk with probability near 0.4 to 0.5 for a few million years, so for the moon rule the draw can use "disk present" at about 45% for the youngest bins and none after about 10 Myr; the disk dust mass is about 0.1 to 0.6 Earth masses at 12 Jupiter masses, a Moon- to Mars-mass reservoir, so the moons it can make are few and small.

3. Checks on the recalled sources and gaps

  • [R1032]: exists as recalled [R1032] and gives the numbers above.
  • "[R1998]": the arXiv search for ejection and moon survival returns [R3640], which has the survival numbers, and Huang and Lai ([R2031], 2025/2026), not a Hong et al. paper. The planned reference is replaced by these two; no number is attributed to Hong.
  • GAP, the disk size limit of an isolated object: the plan text says the zone comes from the object's rotation and its tidal radius. No paper read here gives a radius for a disk around a free-floating planet except the second-hand 15 to 30 AU of a 0.05 solar mass brown dwarf and the unresolved OTS 44. For a bound planet the usual truncation is a fraction of the Hill radius ([R2903] are the sources to read); not read, so it is not used. Until it is read, the disk radius of an isolated object stays a tunable stand-in in the gap plan registry, marked STAND-IN.
  • GAP, tidal ejection speed: Huang and Lai give the ejection velocity scaling with planet mass and initial semi-major axis (their sections 3 and 4); the figures were not transcribed here because GEN.557 has not asked for them.
