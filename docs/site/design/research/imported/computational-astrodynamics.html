Galactic Potential Modeling and Rotation Curve Equilibrium

In discrete \(N\)-body simulations and localized spatial stencil implementations, calculating gravitational acceleration solely from immediate neighbor point masses and an isolated central supermassive black hole induces rapid, unphysical disintegration of stellar orbits. Within disk galaxies, the aggregate baryonic matter distributed throughout the thin and thick disks, the central stellar bulge, and the extended dark matter halo dominates the large-scale gravitational field. A star placed at the solar galactocentric radius (\(R_0 \approx 8.128\text{--}8.200\text{ kpc}\)) with an observed circular velocity of \(v_\phi \approx 229\text{--}232\text{ km/s}\) experiences insufficient inward radial acceleration if only the central black hole (\(M_{\bullet} \approx 4.1 \times 10^6\text{ M}_\odot\)) and immediate stellar neighbors are evaluated. Because the central compact object accounts for less than 0.005% of the total enclosed dynamical mass within the solar circle, the star experiences a severe centrifugal imbalance, causing an artificial hyperbolic escape from the galaxy.

To sustain stable equilibrium rotation curves over multi-gigayear dynamical epochs, the simulation architecture must model the smooth background galactic potential analytically, superimposing localized point-mass interactions as higher-order gravitational perturbations. The galactic potential field is formulated as a three-component axisymmetric composite potential:

\[\Phi_{\text{gal}}(R, z) = \Phi_{\text{bulge}}(r) + \Phi_{\text{disk}}(R, z) + \Phi_{\text{halo}}(r)\]

where \(R = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\) is the Galactocentric cylindrical radius, \(z\) is the vertical height above the galactic midplane, and \(r = \sqrt{R^2 + z^2} = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}\) is the spherical Galactocentric radius.

The central stellar concentration is modeled using the Hernquist potential, which reproduces the de Vaucouleurs \(R^{1/4}\) surface brightness profile of galactic spheroids while providing smooth, singularity-free analytical derivatives:

\[\Phi_{\text{bulge}}(r) = -\frac{G M_{\text{b}}}{r + c_{\text{b}}}\]

where \(M_{\text{b}}\) represents the total bulge mass and \(c_{\text{b}}\) is the Hernquist core scale radius. The gravitational acceleration vector generated by the bulge acting on a position vector \(\vec{r} = [x, y, z]^T\) evaluates to:

\[\vec{a}_{\text{bulge}}(\vec{r}) = -\nabla \Phi_{\text{bulge}}(r) = -\frac{G M_{\text{b}}}{(r + c_{\text{b}})^2} \frac{\vec{r}}{r}\]

The flattened stellar disk is represented via the Miyamoto-Nagai potential, which bridges infinitesimally thin Kuzmin disks and spherical Plummer models through independent radial and vertical scale parameters:

\[\Phi_{\text{disk}}(R, z) = -\frac{G M_{\text{d}}}{\sqrt{R^2 + \left(a_{\text{d}} + \sqrt{z^2 + b_{\text{d}}^2}\right)^2}}\]

where \(M_{\text{d}}\) is the total disk mass, \(a_{\text{d}}\) is the radial scale length, and \(b_{\text{d}}\) is the vertical scale height. The components of the acceleration field in cylindrical coordinates evaluate to:

\[a_{\text{disk}, R} = -\frac{\partial \Phi_{\text{disk}}}{\partial R} = -\frac{G M_{\text{d}} R}{\left[R^2 + \left(a_{\text{d}} + \sqrt{z^2 + b_{\text{d}}^2}\right)^2\right]^{3/2}}\]

\[a_{\text{disk}, z} = -\frac{\partial \Phi_{\text{disk}}}{\partial z} = -\frac{G M_{\text{d}} z \left(a_{\text{d}} + \sqrt{z^2 + b_{\text{d}}^2}\right)}{\sqrt{z^2 + b_{\text{d}}^2} \left[R^2 + \left(a_{\text{d}} + \sqrt{z^2 + b_{\text{d}}^2}\right)^2\right]^{3/2}}\]

Transforming the radial component into global Cartesian coordinates yields the midplane acceleration terms \(a_{\text{disk}, x} = a_{\text{disk}, R} (x / R)\) and \(a_{\text{disk}, y} = a_{\text{disk}, R} (y / R)\).

The dark matter halo is modeled via the Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) profile derived from cosmological cold dark matter assembly simulations:

\[\Phi_{\text{halo}}(r) = -\frac{G M_{\text{h}}}{r} \ln\left(1 + \frac{r}{r_{\text{h}}}\right)\]

where \(M_{\text{h}}\) is the characteristic halo mass parameter and \(r_{\text{h}}\) is the halo scale radius. Setting the dimensionless radius variable \(u = r / r_{\text{h}}\), the inward gravitational acceleration is:

\[\vec{a}_{\text{halo}}(\vec{r}) = -\frac{G M_{\text{h}}}{r^3} \left[\ln(1 + u) - \frac{u}{1 + u}\right] \vec{r}\]

For computational architectures prioritizing execution throughput over cosmological density matching at large radii, an alternative flattened logarithmic halo provides an asymptotic circular velocity \(v_0\) without evaluating transcendental logarithmic series:

\[\Phi_{\text{log}}(R, z) = \frac{1}{2} v_0^2 \ln\left(R_{\text{c}}^2 + R^2 + \frac{z^2}{q^2}\right)\]

where \(R_{\text{c}}\) is the core radius and \(q\) parameterizes halo flattening along the symmetry axis.

The circular velocity curve \(v_{\text{circ}}(R)\) governing a test mass in the galactic midplane ($z = 0$) balances the sum of the inward radial accelerations against centrifugal acceleration, satisfying \(v_{\text{circ}}(R) = \sqrt{R |\partial \Phi_{\text{gal}} / \partial R|_{z=0}} = \sqrt{v_{\text{b}}^2(R) + v_{\text{d}}^2(R) + v_{\text{h}}^2(R)}\). Evaluating the components in galactic units, where the gravitational constant is \(G = 4.300917 \times 10^{-6}\text{ kpc}\cdot(\text{km/s})^2 \cdot \text{M}_\odot^{-1}\), the component contributions are:

\[v_{\text{b}}^2(R) = \frac{G M_{\text{b}} R}{(R + c_{\text{b}})^2}, \quad v_{\text{d}}^2(R) = \frac{G M_{\text{d}} R^2}{\left[R^2 + (a_{\text{d}} + b_{\text{d}})^2\right]^{3/2}}, \quad v_{\text{h}}^2(R) = \frac{G M_{\text{h}}}{R} \left[\ln\left(1 + \frac{R}{r_{\text{h}}}\right) - \frac{R/r_{\text{h}}}{1 + R/r_{\text{h}}}\right]\]

Applying the baseline parameters yields \(v_{\text{circ}}(8.128\text{ kpc}) = 229.27\text{ km/s}\) (\(v_{\text{b}} = 68.5\text{ km/s}\), \(v_{\text{d}} = 163.6\text{ km/s}\), \(v_{\text{h}} = 145.3\text{ km/s}\)), matching empirical kinematic constraints established by Gaia DR3, LAMOST, and DESI surveys (\(V_{\text{LSR}} \approx 229\text{--}232\text{ km/s}\)). Across the radial profile from 4 kpc to 20 kpc, the rotation curve remains flat within a narrow velocity band (213–231 km/s). Incorporating this global potential guarantees that stellar orbits retain their Galactocentric radii during long-term integration without unphysical numerical dispersion, while nearby stars resolved within the local spatial stencil are superimposed as perturbation vectors.

Astrodynamical Reference Frame Conventions and Coordinate Transformations

Hierarchical astrodynamics engines must maintain distinct coordinate frames to prevent translational and rotational cross-contamination between galactic orbits and internal planetary systems. Spatial states must transform unambiguously between the global Galactocentric frame, the sector spatial indexing partitioning, and the local stellar system frames.

The master inertial reference frame is the Galactic Centric Inertial Frame (GCIF). Its origin is fixed at the dynamical center of mass of the galaxy, anchored at the coordinates of the Tier-0 central supermassive black hole. The fundamental reference plane is the galactic midplane ($z = 0$). The unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{\text{gal}}\) points along the north galactic pole, parallel to the total angular momentum vector of the galactic disk. The unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{gal}}\) points from the galactic center toward an established primary reference direction in the disk, and \(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{\text{gal}} = \hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{\text{gal}} \times \hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{gal}}\) completes the right-handed Cartesian triad.

The sector frame serves as a spatial indexing grid used by broadphase collision routines and localized gravitational queries. Whether parameterized as cylindrical shells or cubic cells (\(L_{\text{sec}} = 4\text{ pc}\)), the axes of each sector frame remain strictly aligned with the GCIF, satisfying \(\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{sec}} \parallel \hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{gal}}\), \(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{\text{sec}} \parallel \hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{\text{gal}}\), and \(\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{\text{sec}} \parallel \hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{\text{gal}}\). The origin of sector \(S\) is defined by its spatial bounding minimum \(\vec{p}_{\text{sec\_min}}\). Transforming a position vector \(\vec{r}_{\text{gal}}\) into the local sector frame involves an unrotated spatial translation:

\[\vec{r}_{\text{sec}} = \vec{r}_{\text{gal}} - \vec{p}_{\text{sec\_min}}\]

Because rotation tensors are omitted at the sector boundary level, gravitational vectors computed within stencil neighbor buffers can be summed via linear superposition without incurring coordinate rotation overhead.

For star systems containing bound planetary and minor-body hierarchies, positions must be evaluated in a primary-centered frame to maintain Keplerian orbital precision. The origin of the stellar system frame is fixed at the host star's barycenter \(\vec{p}_*(t)\). The physical reference plane is defined as the star's equatorial plane, establishing the system's \(z\)-axis along the stellar spin axis. In accordance with International Astronomical Union (IAU) Working Group on Cartographic Coordinates conventions, the rotational orientation of a star is defined by the Right Ascension (\(\alpha_*\)) and Declination (\(\delta_*\)) of its north pole of rotation relative to the reference frame:

\[\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{sys}} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_* = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\delta_* \cos\alpha_* \\ \cos\delta_* \sin\alpha_* \\ \sin\delta_* \end{bmatrix}\]

To prevent coordinate indeterminacy when constructing the orthogonal equatorial plane, the node line where the stellar equator intersects the galactic midplane defines the system's \(x\)-axis:

\[\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{sys}} = \frac{\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{\text{gal}} \times \hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{sys}}}{\|\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{\text{gal}} \times \hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{sys}}\|} = \begin{bmatrix} -\sin\alpha_* \\ \cos\alpha_* \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\]

If the stellar rotation axis aligns exactly with the galactic pole (\(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{sys}} = \pm \hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{\text{gal}}\)), the cross product vanishes. The engine resolves this degenerate limit by setting \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{sys}} = \hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{gal}}\). The system's \(y\)-axis is completed by:

\[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{sys}} = \hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{sys}} \times \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{sys}} = \begin{bmatrix} -\sin\delta_* \cos\alpha_* \\ -\sin\delta_* \sin\alpha_* \\ \cos\delta_* \end{bmatrix}\]

The orthogonal direction cosine matrix \(\mathbf{R}_{\text{gal}\to\text{sys}}\) mapping coordinates from the global galactic frame into the stellar equatorial frame is:

\[\mathbf{R}_{\text{gal}\to\text{sys}} = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{sys}}^T \\ \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{sys}}^T \\ \hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{sys}}^T \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -\sin\alpha_* & \cos\alpha_* & 0 \\ -\sin\delta_* \cos\alpha_* & -\sin\delta_* \sin\alpha_* & \cos\delta_* \\ \cos\delta_* \cos\alpha_* & \cos\delta_* \sin\alpha_* & \sin\delta_* \end{bmatrix}\]

Given an entity's global state \((\vec{p}_{\text{gal}}, \vec{v}_{\text{gal}})\) and the host star's state \((\vec{p}_*, \vec{v}_*)\), transformations to and from the system frame follow:

\[\vec{r}_{\text{sys}} = \mathbf{R}_{\text{gal}\to\text{sys}} (\vec{p}_{\text{gal}} - \vec{p}_*), \quad \vec{v}_{\text{sys}} = \mathbf{R}_{\text{gal}\to\text{sys}} (\vec{v}_{\text{gal}} - \vec{v}_*)\]

\[\vec{p}_{\text{gal}} = \vec{p}_* + \mathbf{R}_{\text{gal}\to\text{sys}}^T \vec{r}_{\text{sys}}, \quad \vec{v}_{\text{gal}} = \vec{v}_* + \mathbf{R}_{\text{gal}\to\text{sys}}^T \vec{v}_{\text{sys}}\]

Under this transformation, planetary inclinations (\(i\)) and axial obliquities (\(\varepsilon\)) represent physical angles measured relative to the host star's equatorial plane, eliminating coordinate distortions caused by arbitrary system orientations in the galaxy.

Mathematical Formalization of Core Orbital Solvers

The master simulation pipeline calls three primary orbital mechanics routines: PropagateKeplerianOrbit, ComputeTwoBodyHyperbolicDeflection, and ResolveCloseEncounterMicroPass. These routines require rigorous mathematical definitions to preserve symplectic properties, prevent energy dissipation, and conserve momentum across multi-body encounters.

Universal Variable Keplerian Orbit Propagation

Classical anomaly solvers break down near parabolic trajectories because the semi-major axis \(a\) diverges to infinity (\(a \to \infty\)), causing mean motion \(n = \sqrt{\mu / a^3}\) to vanish. The routine PropagateKeplerianOrbit solves this via the Universal Variable formulation based on Stumpff functions, handling circular, elliptical, parabolic, and hyperbolic orbits through a single unified algorithm.

Given the central gravitational parameter \(\mu = G(M_* + m)\), initial position \(\vec{r}_0\), initial velocity \(\vec{v}_0\), and time interval \(\Delta t\), the specific orbital energy dictates the reciprocal semi-major axis \(\alpha\):

\[r_0 = \Vert{}\vec{r}_0\Vert{}, \quad v_0 = \Vert{}\vec{v}_0\Vert{}, \quad \alpha = \frac{2}{r_0} - \frac{v_0^2}{\mu} = \frac{1}{a}\]

The Stumpff functions \(c_k(z)\) are defined through the general series expansion:

\[c_k(z) = \sum_{m=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^m z^m}{(2m + k)!}\]

In floating-point implementations, the zero-order through third-order functions are evaluated using closed-form trigonometric or hyperbolic expressions depending on the sign of \(z = \alpha \chi^2\):

\[c_0(z) = \begin{cases} \cos\sqrt{z} & z > 0 \\ 1 & z = 0 \\ \cosh\sqrt{-z} & z < 0 \end{cases}, \quad c_1(z) = \begin{cases} \frac{\sin\sqrt{z}}{\sqrt{z}} & z > 0 \\ 1 & z = 0 \\ \frac{\sinh\sqrt{-z}}{\sqrt{-z}} & z < 0 \end{cases}\]

\[c_2(z) = \begin{cases} \frac{1 - \cos\sqrt{z}}{z} & z > 0 \\ \frac{1}{2} & z = 0 \\ \frac{\cosh\sqrt{-z} - 1}{-z} & z < 0 \end{cases}, \quad c_3(z) = \begin{cases} \frac{\sqrt{z} - \sin\sqrt{z}}{z^{3/2}} & z > 0 \\ \frac{1}{6} & z = 0 \\ \frac{\sinh\sqrt{-z} - \sqrt{-z}}{(-z)^{3/2}} & z < 0 \end{cases}\]

The universal anomaly \(\chi\) satisfies the Universal Kepler Equation:

\[F(\chi) = \frac{\vec{r}_0 \cdot \vec{v}_0}{\sqrt{\mu}} \chi^2 c_2(\alpha \chi^2) + (1 - \alpha r_0) \chi^3 c_3(\alpha \chi^2) + r_0 \chi c_1(\alpha \chi^2) - \sqrt{\mu} \Delta t = 0\]

The first two analytical derivatives with respect to \(\chi\) are:

\[F'(\chi) = \frac{\vec{r}_0 \cdot \vec{v}_0}{\sqrt{\mu}} \chi \left[1 - \alpha \chi^2 c_3(\alpha \chi^2)\right] + (1 - \alpha r_0) \chi^2 c_2(\alpha \chi^2) + r_0 c_0(\alpha \chi^2) = r(\chi)\]

\[F''(\chi) = \frac{\vec{r}_0 \cdot \vec{v}_0}{\sqrt{\mu}} \left[1 - \alpha \chi^2 c_2(\alpha \chi^2)\right] + (1 - \alpha r_0) \chi c_1(\alpha \chi^2)\]

An initial iterate \(\chi_0\) is generated based on orbital eccentricity:

\[\chi_0 = \begin{cases} \alpha \sqrt{\mu} \Delta t & \alpha > 0.15 \text{ (Elliptic)} \\ \frac{\sqrt{\mu} \Delta t}{r_0} & \vert{}\alpha\vert{} \le 0.15 \text{ (Near-Parabolic)} \\ \text{sign}(\Delta t) \sqrt{-\frac{1}{\alpha}} \ln\left( \frac{-2 \mu \alpha \Delta t}{\vec{r}_0 \cdot \vec{v}_0 + \sqrt{-\mu/\alpha} (1 - \alpha r_0)} \right) & \alpha < -0.15 \text{ (Hyperbolic)} \end{cases}\]

The root \(\chi^*\) is solved to machine precision (\(\varepsilon \approx 10^{-12}\)) using Halley's third-order iterative method:

\[\chi_{n+1} = \chi_n - \frac{F(\chi_n)}{F'(\chi_n) - \frac{F''(\chi_n) F(\chi_n)}{2 F'(\chi_n)}}\]

Once converged, the updated state vector \((\vec{r}, \vec{v})\) is evaluated via the Lagrange \(f\) and \(g\) coefficients:

\[f = 1 - \frac{\chi^2}{r_0} c_2(\alpha \chi^2), \quad g = \Delta t - \frac{\chi^3}{\sqrt{\mu}} c_3(\alpha \chi^2)\]

\[\vec{r} = f \vec{r}_0 + g \vec{v}_0, \quad r = \Vert{}\vec{r}\Vert{}\]

\[\dot{f} = \frac{\sqrt{\mu}}{r r_0} \chi \left[\alpha \chi^2 c_3(\alpha \chi^2) - 1\right], \quad \dot{g} = 1 - \frac{\chi^2}{r} c_2(\alpha \chi^2)\]

\[\vec{v} = \dot{f} \vec{r}_0 + \dot{g} \vec{v}_0\]

Because the coefficients satisfy the identity \(f \dot{g} - \dot{f} g = 1\) in exact arithmetic, phase-space volume and orbital energy are conserved across arbitrary propagation baselines.

Two-Body Hyperbolic Deflection Kinematics

When an unbound body encounters a secondary mass at high relative velocity without becoming gravitationally bound, ComputeTwoBodyHyperbolicDeflection evaluates the asymptotic flyby turning angle analytically. Given interacting masses \(M_1\) and \(M_2\), the combined gravitational parameter is \(\mu_{\text{enc}} = G(M_1 + M_2)\), with initial positions \(\vec{p}_1, \vec{p}_2\) and velocities \(\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2\). Transforming into the center-of-mass frame:

\[\vec{r}_{\text{rel}} = \vec{p}_2 - \vec{p}_1, \quad \vec{v}_{\text{rel}} = \vec{v}_2 - \vec{v}_1, \quad v_\infty = \Vert{}\vec{v}_{\text{rel}}\Vert{}\]

The specific relative angular momentum vector is:

\[\vec{h}_{\text{enc}} = \vec{r}_{\text{rel}} \times \vec{v}_{\text{rel}}, \quad h_{\text{enc}} = \Vert{}\vec{h}_{\text{enc}}\Vert{}\]

The impact parameter \(b\) and hyperbolic semi-major axis \(a_{\text{hyp}}\) are \(b = h_{\text{enc}} / v_\infty\) and \(a_{\text{hyp}} = \mu_{\text{enc}} / v_\infty^2\). The orbital eccentricity of the unbound encounter evaluates to:

\[e_{\text{hyp}} = \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{b}{a_{\text{hyp}}}\right)^2} = \sqrt{1 + \frac{h_{\text{enc}}^2 v_\infty^2}{\mu_{\text{enc}}^2}}\]

The asymptotic turning angle \(\delta_{\text{infl}}\) between incoming and outgoing velocity vectors is:

\[\sin\left(\frac{\delta_{\text{infl}}}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{e_{\text{hyp}}} \implies \delta_{\text{infl}} = 2 \arcsin\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \left(b v_\infty^2 / \mu_{\text{enc}}\right)^2}}\right)\]

The rotation axis perpendicular to the encounter plane is defined by the unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{enc}} = \vec{h}_{\text{enc}} / h_{\text{enc}}\). Using Rodrigues' rotation formula, the outgoing relative asymptotic velocity vector \(\vec{v}_{\text{rel}}^+\) is:

\[\vec{v}_{\text{rel}}^+ = \vec{v}_{\text{rel}} \cos\delta_{\text{infl}} + \left(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{enc}} \times \vec{v}_{\text{rel}}\right) \sin\delta_{\text{infl}} + \hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{enc}} \left(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{enc}} \cdot \vec{v}_{\text{rel}}\right)(1 - \cos\delta_{\text{infl}})\]

Because \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{enc}} \cdot \vec{v}_{\text{rel}} = 0\), the scalar projection term vanishes identically. The velocity variation vector imparted to the relative trajectory is \(\Delta \vec{v}_{\text{rel}} = \vec{v}_{\text{rel}}^+ - \vec{v}_{\text{rel}}\). Conservation of linear momentum dictates the final inertial velocity updates staged for both bodies:

\[\vec{v}_1^{\text{new}} = \vec{v}_1 - \left(\frac{M_2}{M_1 + M_2}\right) \Delta \vec{v}_{\text{rel}}, \quad \vec{v}_2^{\text{new}} = \vec{v}_2 + \left(\frac{M_1}{M_1 + M_2}\right) \Delta \vec{v}_{\text{rel}}\]

Encounter Resolution and Adaptive Micro-Pass Architecture

When an interloper crosses into another body's mutual Hill sphere (\(r_{\text{sep}} \le r_{\text{hill, mutual}}\)), the macro-step Velocity Verlet integrator suffers severe truncation error due to rapid changes in gravitational jerk. Under these conditions, the macro integration step \(\Delta t\) is suspended for that pair, and ResolveCloseEncounterMicroPass executes an adaptive sub-cycling integration loop. The encounter sub-step \(\delta t_{\text{micro}}\) is calculated from the local orbital frequency:

\[\delta t_{\text{micro}} = \eta \sqrt{\frac{r_{\text{sep}}^3}{G(M_1 + M_2)}}\]

where \(\eta \in [0.01, 0.05]\) is an accuracy parameter controlling maximum angular advance per sub-step. The positions and velocities of both bodies are advanced from \(t_0\) to \(t_0 + \Delta t\) over \(N = \lceil \Delta t / \delta t_{\text{micro}} \rceil\) sub-steps using an energy-conserving 4th-order Hermite or Yoshida symplectic integrator. At each micro-step, mutual separation is evaluated against the physical contact cross-section and the fluid Roche limit:

\[d_{\text{roche}} = 2.44 R_1 \left(\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2}\right)^{1/3}\]

If \(r_{\text{sep}} \le d_{\text{roche}}\) and \(M_1 > M_2\), tidal forces disrupt the secondary mass, flagging it for deletion and staging ring debris formation. If \(r_{\text{sep}} \le R_1 + R_2\), an inelastic merger coalescence executes immediately.

Edge Cases, Singularity Elimination, and Nonsingular Orbital Elements

Representing orbits via classical Keplerian orbital elements \((a, e, i, \Omega, \omega, \nu)\) introduces mathematical singularities in common astrophysical regimes. For circular orbits (\(e \to 0\)), the periapsis vector degenerates, leaving the argument of periapsis \(\omega\) and true anomaly \(\nu\) undefined. For equatorial orbits (\(i \to 0\)), the line of nodes vanishes, leaving the Right Ascension of the Ascending Node \(\Omega\) and \(\omega\) undefined. Furthermore, retrograde orbits (\(i \to \pi\)) exhibit coordinate singularities in standard spherical projections.

To support circular orbits ($e = 0$) and co-planar equatorial systems ($i = 0$) without branching logic or numerical divide-by-zero errors, the engine stores and integrates internal orbits using Modified Equinoctial Elements (MEE). The six-dimensional element state vector is defined as:

\[\mathbf{w} = \begin{bmatrix} p \\ f \\ g \\ h \\ k \\ L \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a(1 - e^2) \\ e \cos(\omega + \Omega) \\ e \sin(\omega + \Omega) \\ \tan(i / 2) \cos\Omega \\ \tan(i / 2) \sin\Omega \\ \Omega + \omega + \nu \end{bmatrix}\]

where \(p\) is the semi-latus rectum, $(f, g)$ model eccentricity projected onto the equinoctial frame, $(h, k)$ represent orbital plane tilt, and \(L\) is the true longitude. This representation is nonsingular for all eccentricities \(e \ge 0\) and all inclinations \(i \in [0, \pi)\), encountering a coordinate singularity only for exactly retrograde equatorial trajectories (\(i = \pi\)).

Converting an instantaneous Cartesian state \((\vec{r}, \vec{v})\) into MEE begins by determining specific angular momentum \(\vec{h} = \vec{r} \times \vec{v}\), its magnitude \(h = \Vert{}\vec{h}\Vert{}\), and unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf{h}} = \vec{h} / h = [h_x, h_y, h_z]^T\). The semi-latus rectum is \(p = h^2 / \mu\). The inclination components evaluate to:

\[k_{\text{elem}} = \frac{h_x}{1 + h_z}, \quad h_{\text{elem}} = -\frac{h_y}{1 + h_z}\]

The orthonormal equinoctial basis vectors \((\hat{\mathbf{f}}, \hat{\mathbf{g}})\) spanning the orbital plane are constructed without evaluating trigonometric functions:

\[s^2 = 1 + h_{\text{elem}}^2 + k_{\text{elem}}^2\]

\[\hat{\mathbf{f}} = \frac{1}{s^2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 - k_{\text{elem}}^2 + h_{\text{elem}}^2 \\ 2 k_{\text{elem}} h_{\text{elem}} \\ -2 k_{\text{elem}} \end{bmatrix}, \quad \hat{\mathbf{g}} = \frac{1}{s^2} \begin{bmatrix} 2 k_{\text{elem}} h_{\text{elem}} \\ 1 + k_{\text{elem}}^2 - h_{\text{elem}}^2 \\ 2 h_{\text{elem}} \end{bmatrix}\]

The eccentricity vector \(\vec{e}\) is calculated via the Laplace-Runge-Lenz formulation:

\[\vec{e} = \frac{\vec{v} \times \vec{h}}{\mu} - \frac{\vec{r}}{\Vert{}\vec{r}\Vert{}}\]

Projecting \(\vec{e}\) onto the equinoctial basis yields the nonsingular components \(f = \vec{e} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{f}}\) and \(g = \vec{e} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{g}}\). The true longitude \(L\) is determined from the coordinates in the equinoctial frame using \(X = \vec{r} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{f}}\), \(Y = \vec{r} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{g}}\), and \(L = \text{atan2}(Y, X)\).

Reconstructing Cartesian coordinates from the MEE vector $(p, f, g, h, k, L)$ proceeds by evaluating the basis vectors \(\hat{\mathbf{f}}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}\) using $(h, k)$. The orbital radius \(r\) is derived from the conic polar equation:

\[w = 1 + f \cos L + g \sin L, \quad r = \frac{p}{w}\]

Cartesian position is reconstructed as \(\vec{r} = (r \cos L) \hat{\mathbf{f}} + (r \sin L) \hat{\mathbf{g}}\). Cartesian velocity evaluates directly to:

\[\vec{v} = -\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{p}} (g + \sin L) \hat{\mathbf{f}} + \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{p}} (f + \cos L) \hat{\mathbf{g}}\]

Round-trip conversions between Cartesian vectors and Modified Equinoctial Elements retain double-precision fidelity across all inclination angles, achieving forward position errors below \(10^{-11}\text{ m}\).

Near the galactic center where \(r \to 0\), point-mass acceleration evaluates to unphysically large values under standard Newtonian formulations. To prevent infinite force spikes without corrupting outer orbits, point-mass evaluations incorporate Plummer softening length \(\epsilon = 1.0\text{ pc}\), replacing raw \(1/r^2\) forces with \(a(r) = G M r / (r^2 + \epsilon^2)^{3/2}\). This guarantees that net acceleration vanishes smoothly at the galactic origin (\(\lim_{r\to 0} a(r) = 0\)), preserving numerical stability in core stellar clusters.

Large Macro-Step Integration, Temporal Baselines, and Continuous Collision Sweeping

Integrating trajectories over large macro-steps (\(\Delta t = 1.0\text{ in-game year} \approx 3.15576 \times 10^7\text{ s}\)) produces numerical challenges when objects have short orbital periods. For inner planets and moons, the integration step can exceed the orbital period by orders of magnitude (\(\Delta t > P_{\text{orb}}\)). For close-in bodies, applying numerical integrators like Velocity Verlet directly across these intervals produces catastrophic orbital energy growth, driving bound worlds into unphysical parabolic ejections within a few updates.

The simulation handles this through a two-phase hierarchical architecture. In Phase A, the parent star's position is integrated within the global galactic potential via Velocity Verlet. The displacement vector \(\Delta \vec{p}_* = \vec{p}_*(t + \Delta t) - \vec{p}_*(t)\) is added directly to all child planet and satellite positions, translating the internal system rigidly to maintain its spatial configuration. In Phase B, bound child orbits are propagated analytically using mean anomaly phase wrapping. The mean motion \(n\) is calculated from the semi-major axis as \(n = \sqrt{\mu / a^3}\), and the updated mean anomaly \(M\) after interval \(\Delta t\) is computed via modular arithmetic:

\[M(t + \Delta t) = \left(M(t) + n \Delta t\right) \pmod{2\pi}\]

Because the orbit advances along an exact Keplerian ellipse, energy is conserved without numerical drift, remaining stable for arbitrarily large steps \(\Delta t / P_{\text{orb}} \gg 1\). To eliminate secular phase errors across successive nightly runs, all orbital anomalies, stellar orientations, and ephemeris baselines are synchronized to the standard J2000.0 epoch (\(T_0 = 2451545.0\text{ Barycentric Dynamical Time}\)), matching IAU conventions.

Discrete distance checks run only at interval endpoints can miss close encounters, allowing fast-moving bodies to pass through one another undetected. The simulation resolves this using Continuous Collision Detection (CCD), evaluating capsule intersections along swept trajectories over \(\tau \in [0, \Delta t]\). Let two bodies move with initial positions \(\vec{p}_1, \vec{p}_2\) and velocities \(\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2\). Defining relative displacement \(\Delta \vec{p}_0 = \vec{p}_1 - \vec{p}_2\) and relative velocity \(\Delta \vec{v} = \vec{v}_1 - \vec{v}_2\), the squared separation as a function of time \(\tau\) is quadratic:

\[S(\tau) = \Vert{}\Delta \vec{p}_0 + \Delta \vec{v} \tau\Vert{}^2 = A \tau^2 + B \tau + C\]

\[A = \Delta \vec{v} \cdot \Delta \vec{v}, \quad B = 2 (\Delta \vec{p}_0 \cdot \Delta \vec{v}), \quad C = \Delta \vec{p}_0 \cdot \Delta \vec{p}_0\]

The unconstrained time of closest approach is \(\tau_{\text{min}} = -B / (2 A)\). Physical collision occurs if the minimum separation falls within the gravitationally focused interaction cross-section. Gravitational focusing expands the geometric collision radius \(R_{\text{geom}} = R_1 + R_2\) based on mutual escape velocity:

\[v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 G(M_1 + M_2)}{R_1 + R_2}}, \quad R_{\text{eff}} = (R_1 + R_2) \sqrt{1 + \frac{v_{\text{esc}}^2}{\max(\Vert{}\Delta \vec{v}\Vert{}^2, 1.0)}}\]

Contact occurs within interval \([0, \Delta t]\) if the quadratic discriminant satisfies \(D = B^2 - 4 A (C - R_{\text{eff}}^2) \ge 0\) and the earliest real entry root falls within the time step:

\[\tau_{\text{impact}} = \frac{-B - \sqrt{D}}{2 A}, \quad 0 \le \tau_{\text{impact}} \le \Delta t\]

When contact is detected, an inelastic merger executes at \(\tau_{\text{impact}}\). Conserving mass and linear momentum, the state of the coalesced body evaluates to:

\[M_{\text{new}} = M_1 + M_2, \quad \vec{p}_{\text{new}} = \vec{p}_1 + \vec{v}_1 \tau_{\text{impact}}\]

\[\vec{v}_{\text{new}} = \frac{M_1 \vec{v}_1 + M_2 \vec{v}_2}{M_1 + M_2}, \quad R_{\text{new}} = \left(R_1^3 + R_2^3\right)^{1/3}\]

The consumed secondary body is flagged for deletion, and affected sectors are marked dirty for point-mass table re-indexing.

The spatial partitioning divides the galaxy into uniform cubic cells of size \(L_{\text{sec}} = 4.0\text{ pc}\) (\(1.234 \times 10^{17}\text{ m}\)), replacing cylindrical geometries to avoid wedge-shaped volume degeneracies near the core. Sectors are indexed in Cartesian space using global integer coordinates \(i_x = \lfloor x_{\text{gal}} / L_{\text{sec}} \rfloor\), \(i_y = \lfloor y_{\text{gal}} / L_{\text{sec}} \rfloor\), and \(i_z = \lfloor z_{\text{gal}} / L_{\text{sec}} \rfloor\). Each cell key is encoded via a 64-bit Morton space-filling curve (Z-order hash), mapping 3D spatial locality into sequential database memory keys. Gravitational stencil queries resolve the 27 adjacent cubic cells (\(3 \times 3 \times 3\)), bounding the search window to a local sphere of radius \(R_{\text{search}} = \sqrt{G M_{\text{max}} / a_{\text{min}}}\). This ensures short-range point-mass interactions are fully captured across sector boundaries while keeping query complexity bounded at \(\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{local}} \log N_{\text{local}})\).

Synthesis and Structural Recommendations for Implementation

The numerical methods and structural parameters investigated confirm that the proposed configuration provides a mathematically complete foundation for the simulation architecture. Incorporating the composite galactic potential \(\Phi_{\text{gal}} = \Phi_{\text{bulge}} + \Phi_{\text{disk}} + \Phi_{\text{halo}}\) resolves the primary dynamical instability of the engine. By establishing a Hernquist bulge (\(M_{\text{b}} = 1.0 \times 10^{10}\text{ M}_\odot, c_{\text{b}} = 0.5\text{ kpc}\)), a Miyamoto-Nagai disk (\(M_{\text{d}} = 6.8 \times 10^{10}\text{ M}_\odot, a_{\text{d}} = 3.5\text{ kpc}, b_{\text{d}} = 0.3\text{ kpc}\)), and an NFW halo (\(M_{\text{h}} = 5.4 \times 10^{11}\text{ M}_\odot, r_{\text{h}} = 16.0\text{ kpc}\)), the background acceleration sustains the flat 229.3 km/s rotation curve observed at the solar circle, permanently preventing unphysical outward stellar drift.

The missing algorithmic definitions in the codebase are closed by formal mathematical operators. Propagating bound stellar orbits via the universal variable formulation with Stumpff series, solved via Halley's third-order iteration, ensures uniform convergence across elliptical, parabolic, and hyperbolic regimes without analytical singularities. Adopting Modified Equinoctial Elements $(p, f, g, h, k, L)$ eliminates classical geometrical singularities for circular ($e = 0$) and equatorial ($i = 0$) planetary trajectories, eliminating branching overhead in large-scale system processing. Close encounters are resolved by calculating hyperbolic deflection turning angles analytically through Rodrigues' formulation, while deep mutual Hill sphere crossings transition to an adaptive micro-pass sub-cycling integrator governed by local dynamical timescales.

Reference frame ambiguities are eliminated by formalizing the Galactic Centric Inertial Frame as the global datum, aligning sector spatial partitions directly with the galactic axes, and referencing all internal system dynamics to the host star's equatorial plane. Setting the stellar equatorial frame's \(z\)-axis along the stellar spin axis \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_*\) aligns planetary inclinations, obliquities, and stellar oblateness forces with physical rotational axes. Finally, advancing the galaxy by one in-game year per run via host-translation coupled with modular mean anomaly propagation prevents numerical energy dissipation for inner worlds, while swept-sphere continuous collision detection with gravitational focusing preserves momentum during orbital crossings.
