Atmospheric Toxicity, Geochemical Evolution, and Marginal Habitability in Terrestrial Exoplanetary Systems
==========================================================================================================

Evaluating the habitability of an exoplanet requires assessing circumstellar orbital mechanics, planetary geodynamics, and the physiological compatibility of the volatile envelope ([R2281]; [R3986, R3987]). In astrobiology and speculative planetary science, "air" is neither inherently benign nor static; rather, it represents a non-equilibrium chemical reservoir shaped by interior mantle redox thermodynamics, impact delivery, surface-atmosphere geochemical cycles, photochemical networks, and hydrodynamic escape [R0692, R1465, R2544].

An exoplanet possessing surface liquid water within the classical circumstellar habitable zone may nevertheless sustain an atmospheric composition that is toxic to human physiology or completely prohibitive to the origin and macro-evolutionary radiation of complex native organisms [R2281, R3986]. This report examines the fundamental chemical and physical mechanisms governing atmospheric toxicity, the natural endogenic and exogenic processes driving volatile diversification on Earth-sized worlds, and the speculative continuum of marginally habitable planets where severe climatic or atmospheric barriers preclude the spontaneous development of native complex ecosystems, yet permit human survival under targeted technological mitigation [R3986, R4767].

## Thermodynamic and Biochemical Mechanisms of Atmospheric Toxicity

Atmospheric toxicity operates along two distinct vectors: acute human physiological failure modes and universal biochemical constraints that inhibit carbon-based, aqueous organic life [R3986, R3987]. Atmospheric toxicity is fundamentally thermodynamic, driven by the inspired partial pressure ($p_i$) of volatile species, total barometric pressure ($P_{\text{tot}}$), and the resulting redox potentials imposed on living tissues ([R0343]; [R3986, R3987]).

### Human Physiological Boundaries

Human respiration relies on a narrow thermodynamic window of inspired oxygen partial pressure ($p\text{O}_2$) and total ambient pressure to sustain alveolar gas exchange without damaging cellular infrastructure. When ambient barometric pressure drops below the Armstrong limit of approximately $6.3\text{ kPa}$ ($47\text{ mm Hg}$), the total pressure equals the vapor pressure of pure water at human core body temperature ($37^\circ\text{C}$), causing exposed bodily fluids within the alveolar spaces and mucous membranes to undergo spontaneous ebullism. Even above this threshold, a minimum $p\text{O}_2$ of approximately $8.0\text{ to }9.6\text{ kPa}$ ($60\text{ to }72\text{ mm Hg}$) is required to maintain the diffusion gradient across the alveolar-capillary membrane; values falling below this trigger immediate hypoxic syncope, irreversible cerebral ischemia, and cellular death.

Conversely, an overabundance of molecular oxygen induces hyperoxia. Chronic exposure to an inspired $p\text{O}_2$ exceeding $40\text{ to }53\text{ kPa}$ ($300\text{ to }400\text{ mm Hg}$) damages pulmonary capillary endothelial cells via the excessive generation of reactive oxygen species ($\text{ROS}$), including superoxide anions ($\text{O}_2^{\bullet-}$), hydroxyl radicals ($\text{OH}^\bullet$), and hydrogen peroxide ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$). Under hyperbaric regimes where $p\text{O}_2$ surpasses $160\text{ kPa}$, central nervous system toxicity manifests rapidly, causing visual field contraction, diaphragmatic spasms, and tonic-clonic convulsions.

Carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$) acts as a systemic metabolic toxin rather than a simple asphyxiant [R3987]. While ambient Earth conditions expose organisms to roughly $0.04\text{ kPa}$ of $\text{CO}_2$, human physiological homeostasis requires long-term ambient levels to remain below $0.93\text{ kPa}$ ($7\text{ mm Hg}$) to prevent respiratory compensation fatigue [R3986, R3987].

When inspired $p\text{CO}_2$ exceeds $1.0\text{ to }2.0\text{ kPa}$, the diminished diffusion gradient from venous blood to inspired alveolar air produces progressive hypercapnic acidosis, characterized by a dropping blood pH, cellular membrane hyperpolarization, cognitive impairment, and severe headache [R3987]. Sustained exposures exceeding $5.0\text{ kPa}$ induce profound systemic acidosis, compensatory electrolyte shifts, respiratory center depression, narcosis, and fatal cardiac arrhythmias [R3987].

Chemically inert background gases can also induce physical toxicity under hyperbaric surface pressures via inert gas narcosis [R0343]. Governed by the Meyer-Overton correlation, non-polar molecules partition into the hydrophobic lipid bilayer of neuronal membranes in direct proportion to their lipid solubility, swelling the bilayer, altering membrane fluidity, and inhibiting the gating mechanisms of essential ion channels [R0343]. Molecular nitrogen ($\text{N}_2$) induces progressive psychomotor impairment and intellectual deficits at partial pressures exceeding $300\text{ to }400\text{ kPa}$. Argon ($\text{Ar}$), which is twice as soluble in lipids as nitrogen, triggers narcotic impairment at roughly half the partial pressure of $\text{N}_2$, making dense argon-rich envelopes toxic to human cognition even when oxygen ratios are biologically normal [R0343].

### Universal Biological Toxicity Vectors

Beyond mammalian systems, atmospheric constituents can disrupt baseline organic biochemistry by altering environmental pH, inactivating metalloenzymes, or degrading biological polymers [R0692, R3987].

Carbon monoxide ($\text{CO}$) presents a universal physiological barrier to organisms dependent on transition-metal respiratory pigments [R3986]. The carbon monoxide molecule functions as a strong $\pi$-acceptor ligand, binding with high affinity to the ferrous iron ($\text{Fe}^{2+}$) located in the active sites of porphyrin complexes, including hemoglobin, myoglobin, and mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase [R3986]. This binding arrests the aerobic electron transport chain and halts oxidative phosphorylation [R3986, R3987].

While specialized chemolithoautotrophic and anaerobic microorganisms can metabolize $\text{CO}$ through the Wood-Ljungdahl pathway via nickel-iron carbon monoxide dehydrogenase enzymes [R3648], high partial pressures of atmospheric $\text{CO}$ impose an insurmountable evolutionary barrier against the development of aerobic metazoans possessing centralized circulatory frameworks [R3986, R3987].

Reduced sulfur species, most notably hydrogen sulfide ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$), act as potent neurotoxins and cellular poisons by binding the ferric iron ($\text{Fe}^{3+}$) moiety in cytochrome c oxidase, shutting down cellular respiration in aerobic organisms at micromolar concentrations [R3986, R3987]. Although anoxic microbial ecosystems exploit $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ as an electron donor for anoxygenic photosynthesis and sulfate reduction, its atmospheric accumulation acidifies aqueous boundary layers and destroys complex organic matrices [R3986].

Similarly, volcanically outgassed acid volatiles, such as sulfur dioxide ($\text{SO}_2$), hydrogen chloride ($\text{HCl}$), and hydrogen fluoride ($\text{HF}$), dissolve into aqueous droplets and surface reservoirs to produce sulfuric and hydrochloric acids [R3986, R3987]. The resulting environmental proton flux hydrolyzes phosphodiester and peptide bonds, denatures functional tertiary proteins, and drives the pH of open oceans below the threshold required to stabilize mineralized structural shells or biological membranes [R3986, R3987].

## Geochemical and Astrobiological Drivers of Atmospheric Composition

The volatile envelope of a terrestrial exoplanet within the ecosphere is primarily determined by internal mantle thermodynamics, low-temperature water-rock alteration, host star photochemical interactions, and hydrodynamic atmospheric escape ([R0692, R1491, R2544]; [R3986, R3987]).

### Mantle Oxygen Fugacity and Volatile Degassing

The initial speciation of volcanic gases discharged from a planet's interior is dictated by the oxygen fugacity ($f\text{O}_2$) of its upper mantle [R1491, R1953, R3378]. Oxygen fugacity defines the chemical potential of oxygen in equilibrium with a silicate melt, parameterized against solid-state mineral redox buffers such as iron-wüstite ($\text{IW}$) and quartz-fayalite-magnetite ($\text{QFM}$) [R1465, R1953]. When planetary accretion processes and core-mantle differentiation leave a mantle reduced—at or below the $\text{IW}$ buffer ($\Delta\text{IW} \le 0$)—metallic iron remains present in the silicate phase, fixing the escaping volcanic volatile speciation into reduced forms [R1465, R1491]:

$$\Delta\text{IW} \le 0 \implies \text{H}_2,\ \text{CO},\ \text{CH}_4,\ \text{H}_2\text{S}$$

Under these reduced conditions, volcanic degassing supplies little to no direct carbon dioxide or sulfur dioxide, flooding the surface with carbon monoxide, hydrogen, and volatile sulfides [R1465, R1491, R3985].

Only when the mantle undergoes iron disproportionation—wherein ferrous iron ($\text{Fe}^{2+}$) transitions into ferric iron ($\text{Fe}^{3+}$) and metallic iron ($\text{Fe}^0$) under the immense pressures of the lower mantle, with the metal subsequently sequestered into the core—does the upper mantle become oxidized to the $\text{QFM}$ buffer ($\Delta\text{IW} +3.5\text{ to }+4.0$) [R1465].

Basaltic melts ascending from an oxidized mantle switch their volatile yield entirely, outgassing high-mean-molecular-weight atmospheres composed of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{N}_2$, and $\text{SO}_2$ [R1465, R1491, R3378]. If an exoplanet is small or lacks the internal pressure regimes necessary to trigger deep iron disproportionation, its mantle remains perpetually reduced, trapping its secondary atmosphere in an anoxic, $\text{CO}\text{-}$ and $\text{H}_2\text{-rich}$ chemical regime [R1465, R1491].

### Serpentinization and Abiotic Methane Synthesis

Water-rock reactions occurring within oceanic lithospheres supply volatile inputs independently of volcanism [R3648, R4119]. When surface water penetrates deep faults into crustal and upper mantle ultramafic rocks rich in magnesium-iron olivine ($(\text{Mg},\text{Fe})_2\text{SiO}_4$), hydration alters the mineral matrix into serpentine, brucite, and magnetite [R3648, R4119]:

$$3\text{Fe}_2\text{SiO}_4 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow 2\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4 + 3\text{SiO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2$$

This reaction oxidizes ferrous iron into ferric iron, generating large amounts of dissolved molecular hydrogen [R3648, R4119]. In hydrothermal systems where dissolved inorganic carbon is abundant, the hydrogen reduces $\text{CO}_2$ via abiotic Fischer-Tropsch-type ($\text{FTT}$) and Sabatier pathways [R3648, R4119]:

$$\text{CO}_2 + 4\text{H}_2 \longrightarrow \text{CH}_4 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

Although serpentinization can sustain steady-state atmospheric fluxes of $\text{H}_2$ and $\text{CH}_4$, the kinetic limitations of non-biological $\text{FTT}$ catalysis produce substantial quantities of intermediate carbon monoxide [R3985, R4119].

Abiotic planets characterized by serpentinization generate elevated atmospheric $\text{CO}/\text{CH}_4$ ratios ([R2488, R2492]; [R3985]). Conversely, living biospheres utilize metabolic pathways that consume $\text{CO}$ to drive hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis, depleting environmental $\text{CO}$ and generating high atmospheric $\text{CH}_4/\text{CO}$ disequilibrium ([R2488, R2492]; [R3648, R3985]).

### Photochemical Cascades and the Carbon Monoxide Runaway

The spectral energy distribution of the host star directly influences atmospheric equilibrium, particularly for terrestrial planets orbiting M-dwarf stars ([R1511]; [R3986, R3987]; [R4355]). In an atmosphere containing carbon dioxide, far-ultraviolet radiation photolyzes $\text{CO}_2$ into carbon monoxide and atomic oxygen ([R1137, R1511]; [R3986, R3987]):

$$\text{CO}_2 + h\nu (\lambda < 200\text{ nm}) \longrightarrow \text{CO} + \text{O}$$

On Earth, this photolysis is balanced in the troposphere by catalytic oxidation cycles mediated by hydroxyl radicals ($\text{OH}$), which react rapidly with $\text{CO}$ to regenerate $\text{CO}_2$ [R3986, R3987]:

$$\text{CO} + \text{OH} \longrightarrow \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}$$

The continuous formation of $\text{OH}$ radicals relies on the photolysis of ozone ($\text{O}_3$) by near-ultraviolet radiation ($\text{NUV}$, $\lambda \sim 310\text{ nm}$), which yields excited singlet oxygen ($\text{O}(^1\text{D})$) that subsequently cleaves water vapor [R3986, R3987]:

$$\text{O}_3 + h\nu \longrightarrow \text{O}_2 + \text{O}(^1\text{D})$$$$\text{O}(^1\text{D}) + \text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow 2\text{OH}$$

Because M-dwarf stars exhibit high ratios of extreme ultraviolet and far-ultraviolet to near-ultraviolet flux ($\text{FUV}/\text{NUV}$), their planetary atmospheres experience rapid $\text{CO}_2$ photolysis while lacking the $\text{NUV}$ photons needed to generate sufficient $\text{OH}$ sinks ([R3986, R3987]; [R4355, R4356]).

When the abiotic surface outgassing of $\text{CO}$ and $\text{CH}_4$ exceeds the available photochemical $\text{OH}$ recycling capacity, the hydroxyl sink collapses entirely [R1813, R2544]. The planet enters a self-sustaining state termed the _carbon monoxide runaway_, allowing $\text{CO}$ to build to partial pressures between $0.01\text{ and }0.1\text{ bar}$, poisoning the entire ecosphere for aerobic metazoans ([R1511, R1813]; [R3986, R3987]).

### Hydrodynamic and Kinetic Escape Regimes

Planetary atmospheric composition is continually sculpted by thermal and non-thermal escape processes [R0692, R3389]. During the initial several hundred million years of stellar evolution, intense stellar X-ray and extreme ultraviolet ($\text{XUV}$) irradiation heats the planetary thermosphere, initiating hydrodynamic escape [R2769, R3389]. In this regime, the upper atmosphere expands into space as a supersonic planetary wind, carrying light atomic hydrogen along with heavier trace species via collisional drag [R0692, R3389].

As stellar activity declines, escape shifts to the kinetic Jeans regime, where individual atoms in the high-energy Maxwellian tail exceed the escape velocity ($v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2GM/R}$) [R0692, R3389]. While massive, volatile-rich Super-Earths retain light hydrogen envelopes across gigayear timescales, low-mass terrestrial bodies suffer preferential hydrogen loss [R0692, R3518].

If unmitigated by an intrinsic magnetic field, solar wind sputtering, ion pick-up, and charge exchange further erode volatile reservoirs, stripping atmospheres down to thin, dry crustal residues, as observed on Mars [R0692].

## Scientifically Plausible Atmospheric Archetypes in the Ecosphere

Geochemical outgassing, stellar spectral dynamics, and escape processes give rise to several distinct atmospheric archetypes within circumstellar habitable zones [R2769, R3986].

### Archetype A: Oxidized Dense Carbon Dioxide Worlds

Situated near the outer edge of the habitable zone, planets governed by an active carbonate-silicate cycle require high concentrations of greenhouse gases to offset low stellar insolation [R2281, R3986]. As surface temperatures decline, continental weathering rates drop, causing volcanic $\text{CO}_2$ to accumulate in the atmosphere until reaching radiative equilibrium [R2281, R3987].

To prevent global glaciation near the outer boundary of a G- or K-type star's ecosphere, steady-state $\text{CO}_2$ partial pressures must reach $1.0\text{ to }5.0\text{ bar}$ [R3987]. Beyond this point, the atmosphere reaches the maximum greenhouse limit, where the cooling effect of Rayleigh scattering begins to exceed the greenhouse warming capacity of additional $\text{CO}_2$ [R2281, R3986].

While these planets maintain liquid surface oceans and provide viable conditions for chemolithoautotrophic and anaerobic single-celled life, their atmospheric envelopes are toxic to complex metazoans [R2281, R3986, R3991]. Any organisms dependent on aerobic respiration would suffer acute hypercapnia and blood acidification within seconds [R3986, R3987].

### Archetype B: Abiotic Hyperbaric Oxygen Worlds

Terrestrial planets orbiting M-dwarf stars often accumulate massive, abiotic oxygen atmospheres through oceanic photolysis and hydrodynamic hydrogen loss [R2769, R3016, R4356]. Because low-mass M dwarfs remain in an extended, highly luminous pre-main-sequence phase for hundreds of millions of years, planets within the eventual habitable zone spend their early history in a runaway greenhouse state [R2769]. Water vapor fills the stratosphere, where intense stellar XUV flux dissociates $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ molecules into atomic hydrogen and oxygen ([R2769]; [R4355, R4356]).

The light hydrogen escapes into space via hydrodynamic blow-off, leaving large volumes of abiotic oxygen behind [R2769, R4355]. If the crust and mantle become saturated with oxidized iron or lack the active subduction necessary to recycle crustal oxidants into the deep interior, the oxygen sink fails ([R2769]; [R4355, R4356]).

The abiotic $\text{O}_2$ accumulates into hyperbaric envelopes with partial pressures spanning $10\text{ to }100+\text{ bar}$ ([R2769]; [R4355, R4356]). Such environments destroy prebiotic organic compounds through oxidative degradation, precluding the emergence of native life while creating a hyperbaric environment that triggers fatal oxygen toxicity seizures in humans [R1813, R4355].

### Archetype C: Hydrogen-Enriched Atmospheres

Terrestrial planets possessing several Earth masses (Super-Earths) and sub-Neptunes can retain primordial hydrogen-helium envelopes or regenerate secondary $\text{H}_2$ atmospheres via serpentinization and reduced volcanic degassing [R3518, R3991]. In these dense atmospheres, molecular hydrogen generates greenhouse warming through collision-induced absorption ($\text{CIA}$) [R3518, R4770].

When $\text{H}_2$ molecules collide with other molecules of $\text{H}_2$, $\text{N}_2$, or $\text{CO}_2$, transient dipole moments are induced that absorb thermal infrared radiation across the critical $8\text{--}12\ \mu\text{m}$ atmospheric window [R3518, R4770]. This collisional absorption mechanism can warm an exoplanet well beyond the traditional outer edge of the habitable zone ([R3518]; [R3986, R3987]; [R4770]).

In deep ocean variants—the Hycean world archetype—surface water oceans persist underneath a $10\text{ to }100\text{-bar}$ hydrogen envelope [R2804, R3522]. These environments can host anaerobic, extremophilic single-celled organisms, as laboratory tests show that bacteria such as _Escherichia coli_ grow and reproduce under pure $\text{H}_2$ atmospheres [R3991].

However, they are uninhabitable for complex aerobic life and humans due to total anoxia, severe hyperbaric hydrogen narcosis, and high atmospheric density, which causes excessive work of breathing in mammalian lungs [R3522].

### Archetype D: Photochemically Stalled Anoxic Worlds

Planets orbiting early-to-mid M dwarfs that possess moderate outgassing fluxes of $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ can enter a photochemically arrested state [R3986, R3987]. Due to low stellar near-ultraviolet emission, the tropospheric $\text{OH}$ radical sink remains too small to recombine photolyzed $\text{CO}$ back into $\text{CO}_2$ [R3986, R3987].

Carbon monoxide accumulates until reaching photochemical steady state via slower, surface deposition pathways, resulting in atmospheres containing $1\%\text{ to }10\%\ \text{CO}$, $80\%\ \text{N}_2$, and trace levels of $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{CH}_4$ [R1511, R1813, R3986].

These conditions support prebiotic organic synthesis and provide chemical free energy for simple chemotrophic anaerobes [R1813, R3648]. Conversely, the elevated $\text{CO}$ concentrations cause rapid carboxyhemoglobin saturation in complex aerobic organisms, resulting in rapid hypoxia and circulatory collapse [R3986].

## The Marginal Habitability Spectrum: Biogenesis Failure versus Anthropogenic Viability

A central consideration in exoplanetary science is the decoupling between _biogenic habitability_—the capacity of an environment to support spontaneous abiogenesis and evolutionary diversification—and _anthropogenic survivability_, which measures the ability of humans to operate on a surface with simple mechanical mitigation ([R2281]; [R3986, R3987]; [R4767]).

### The Breathing-Mask-Only Paradigm

A common trope in planetary exploration imagines an absolute dichotomy between Earth-like paradise worlds and airless vacuum environments requiring heavy, pressurized space suits. However, geochemical modeling reveals an expansive intermediate class of worlds [R3986, R3987].

If total atmospheric pressure resides between approximately $50\text{ and }250\text{ kPa}$ ($0.5\text{ to }2.5\text{ bar}$), ambient barometric forces prevent bodily fluid ebullism and stave off the mechanical tissue distortion associated with the Armstrong limit [R4767].

If global temperatures simultaneously maintain a range of $273\text{ to }315\text{ K}$ ($0^\circ\text{C}\text{ to }42^\circ\text{C}$), human thermoregulation functions without liquid-cooling garments or active electric heating systems [R4767].

In this regime, mechanical counterpressure suits ($\text{MCP}$ suits) and rigid extravehicular pressure vessels become redundant. The sole barrier to human survival is chemical toxicity or anoxia [R3986, R3987].

On a world with an atmosphere of $90\%\ \text{N}_2$ and $10\%\ \text{CO}_2$ at a surface pressure of $1.0\text{ bar}$, an unprotected human would lose consciousness from hypercapnic acidosis and hypoxia within two minutes [R3986]. Yet, because ambient barometric and thermal conditions are stable, surface exploration requires only an unpressurized garment and a positive-pressure breathing mask providing an Earth-normal gas mixture, bypassing the operational hurdles of space exploration.

### The Habitable Zone for Complex Life Mismatch

The disconnect between planetary habitability and animal biology is most pronounced at the outer edges of habitable zones [R3986]. As demonstrated by Schwieterman et al., the classical habitable zone defines its outer boundary using the maximum greenhouse limit, where dense multi-bar $\text{CO}_2$ atmospheres maintain liquid water [R2281, R3986].

While simple unicellular organisms survive under multi-bar $\text{CO}_2$ pressures, complex aerobic organisms possessing circulatory networks cannot tolerate $p\text{CO}_2$ values above $0.005\text{ to }0.05\text{ bar}$ due to systemic respiratory acidosis and gas exchange failure ([R3986, R3987]; [R3991]).

Consequently, the _Habitable Zone for Complex Life_ ($\text{HZCL}$) spans only $20\%\text{ to }28\%$ of the width of the conventional microbial habitable zone around solar-type G-stars [R3986].

Around M dwarfs, this zone narrows further; photochemical runaway often drives $\text{CO}$ accumulation across the entire habitable zone, creating conditions that are chemically lethal to complex aerobic metazoans despite the presence of stable oceans and thriving microbial mats [R3986].

### Climatic and Density Extremes in Marginal Habitability

Beyond chemical toxicity, planetary environments can be rendered marginally habitable by severe physical extremes that inhibit the development of native life while remaining accessible to human technology:

* **Post-Runaway Desiccated Super-Earths:** A terrestrial planet situated along the inner edge of the habitable zone may experience early oceanic desiccation, leaving behind a hyper-arid "dune world" ([R2281]; [R4355, R4356]). With minimal surface moisture, the atmospheric relative humidity remains very low, which suppresses the runaway moist greenhouse effect and keeps the climate stable despite high stellar insolation [R2281].
  Because open water is absent, the carbonic acid weathering of silicate rocks ceases, allowing volcanic outgassing of $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{SO}_2$ to build a dry, high-pressure atmosphere ([R2281]; [R3986, R3987]). The absence of long-lived open water bodies presents a significant barrier to the abiogenesis of native life [R2281].
  Nonetheless, human operations can function on the surface using particulate-filtering breathing masks, closed-cycle water reclamation, and reflective thermal clothing, avoiding the complex pressurization hardware required in true vacuum environments [R3986].

* **Sub-Baric Ice Worlds:** Low-mass terrestrial bodies ($0.3\text{ to }0.5\ M_\oplus$) orbiting near the middle or outer edges of the habitable zone often lose substantial atmospheric mass through impact erosion and kinetic escape, driving surface pressures down to $10\text{--}20\text{ kPa}$ ($0.1\text{--}0.2\text{ bar}$) [R0692, R2281]. While this pressure remains above the Armstrong limit, the thin atmosphere provides little greenhouse warming, locking the planet into a global Snowball glaciation state [R3991].
  Localized volcanic fissures and subglacial geothermal vents may maintain transient liquid water habitats, but the cold and dry conditions severely limit the complexity of native biospheres [R2997].
  Human survival in this environment does not require heavy, rigid pressure suits; operators can function using heated thermal polar garments and sealed rebreather masks that supply pure oxygen at an ambient partial pressure of $10\text{--}20\text{ kPa}$, matching standard alveolar oxygenation requirements.

* **Hyperbaric Inert Gas Worlds:** Volatile-rich terrestrial planets that accrete large primordial nitrogen inventories or retain significant radiogenic argon can sustain surface pressures of $5.0\text{ to }10.0\text{ bar}$ [R0343, R4770]. Even if temperatures remain clement and carbon dioxide levels are low, the dense atmosphere presents unique physical challenges [R0343, R4767].
  The elevated column weight increases the work of breathing, and the high partial pressures of nitrogen and argon induce inert gas narcosis [R0343].
  While simple native organisms thrive without ill effects, human surface access requires living in hypobaric surface habitats and breathing tailored gas mixtures—such as hydrogen-oxygen ($\text{Hydrox}$) or helium-oxygen ($\text{Heliox}$)—to prevent narcosis during outdoor excursions [R0343].

## Synthesis and Astrobiological Outlook



Planetary habitability is not a binary condition, but an overlapping continuum shaped by mantle thermodynamics, stellar spectral features, and evolutionary biology:

1. **Atmospheric toxicity is fundamentally thermodynamic and physiological.** The ability of a gas to support or extinguish life depends on its partial pressure and biochemical properties. Atmospheric components that sustain microbial metabolisms—such as high concentrations of $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{CO}$—act as neurotoxins and metabolic poisons in complex metazoans by inducing hypercapnic acidosis and ligand binding in respiratory metalloproteins [R3986].

2. **Atmospheres reflect the redox evolution of planetary interiors.** The chemical speciation of a secondary atmosphere is tied to the oxidation state of the underlying silicate mantle. Mantles buffered near the iron-wüstite boundary outgas reduced volatile envelopes rich in $\text{H}_2$, $\text{CO}$, and $\text{H}_2\text{S}$, whereas oxidized mantles produce envelopes dominated by $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{CO}_2$, and $\text{SO}_2$ [R1465, R1491].

3. **Stellar spectral properties drive atmospheric chemical equilibria.** Around M-dwarf stars, a high ratio of extreme ultraviolet to near-ultraviolet flux can suppress the tropospheric $\text{OH}$ radical sink, initiating carbon monoxide runaway, or strip away water inventories to leave behind dense, abiotic oxygen envelopes [R2769, R3986, R4356].

4. **Marginal habitability decouples indigenous biogenesis from human survival.** Environments that prevent the emergence or macro-evolution of native life—including hyper-arid dune worlds, hypercapnic outer-habitable-zone planets, and frozen sub-baric worlds—can remain accessible to human presence [R2281, R3986]. Provided surface pressures remain above the Armstrong limit of $6.3\text{ kPa}$ and temperatures avoid thermal extremes, human exploration avoids the logistical burdens of full-body pressure suits, relying instead on lightweight breathing masks, chemical filtration, and targeted thermal protection to establish a lasting operational footprint.
